The Twelve Labours of Hercules

Twelve Trials. One Hero. Zero Losses.

Poltroweb bonus track number two: a short account of the twelve labours of Hercules, son of Zeus, greatest of the Greek heroes, illustrated with whatever evidence the archives had on file.

King Eurystheus set twelve impossible tasks before him, hoping he would fail. He did not. Beast, river, bird, and border all fell in turn before his strength and his cunning. Read on, and marvel.

Labour I — The Nemean Lion

A lion with a hide no blade could pierce. Hercules found its cave, blocked the second exit, and strangled it bare-handed. He wore its pelt ever after as armour no weapon could match.


Fig. 1 - The lion, stone still and thoroughly subdued.

Labour II — The Lernaean Hydra

Cut off one head, two grew back. Hercules had his nephew Iolaus sear each stump with fire the moment it fell, until the monster ran out of heads to grow.

Labour III — The Ceryneian Hind

A deer sacred to Artemis, too swift to catch and too sacred to harm. Hercules pursued it for a full year before cornering it, unharmed, and carrying it home alive.

[Photo pending. Filed for future archival acquisition.]

Fig. 3 - The Hind, once cornered, considerably calmer.

Labour IV — The Erymanthian Boar

A monstrous boar terrorising Arcadia. Hercules drove it into deep snow, exhausted it, and carried it back alive on his shoulders — a sight that reportedly sent King Eurystheus diving into a storage jar.

[Photo pending. Filed for future archival acquisition.]

Fig. 4 - The Boar, delivered as promised, alive and unimpressed.

Labour V — The Augean Stables

Thirty years of filth, cleared in a single day. Hercules diverted two rivers straight through the stables and let the flood do the work no broom ever could.


Fig. 5 - The rivers, diverted. The stables, finally clean.

Labour VI — The Stymphalian Birds

Man-eating birds with beaks and feathers of bronze, infesting a lake and darkening the sky. Hercules used a pair of bronze rattles from Athena to startle them into flight, then brought them down as they fled.


Fig. 6 - The flock, scattering. The rattle worked exactly as advertised.

Labour VII — The Cretan Bull

A rampaging bull terrorising Crete, breathing fire and flattening everything in its path. Hercules wrestled it to the ground with his bare hands and shipped it back across the sea.


Fig. 7 - The bull, muddied and beaten. The fog adds nothing but atmosphere.

Labour VIII — The Mares of Diomedes

Four horses that fed on human flesh. Hercules fed them their own master, Diomedes, and found them docile ever after.

[Photo pending. Filed for future archival acquisition.]

Fig. 8 - The Mares, fed, tamed, and considerably quieter.

Labour IX — The Belt of Hippolyta

The war belt of Hippolyta, Queen of the Amazons, given as a gift after Hercules won her favour through words rather than the war that treachery nearly caused.


Fig. 9 - The belt, handed over. No war required after all.

Labour X — The Cattle of Geryon

The prized red cattle of Geryon, a giant with three bodies joined at the waist, guarded on a distant island. Hercules felled the giant with a single arrow and herded every last animal home.


Fig. 10 - The herd, accounted for. Not one animal lost on the journey.

Labour XI — The Apples of the Hesperides

Golden apples growing in a garden at the edge of the world, guarded by a hundred-headed dragon. Hercules tricked Atlas into fetching them while he briefly held up the sky in his place.


Fig. 11 - The golden fruit, claimed. The sky, briefly borrowed, returned undamaged.

Labour XII — Cerberus

The final labour: descend into the underworld and drag back its three-headed guardian, using no weapon but bare hands. Hercules did exactly that, presented the beast to a terrified Eurystheus, and returned it to its post unharmed.

[Photo pending. Filed for future archival acquisition.]

Fig. 12 - Cerberus, returned to his post. The underworld did not ask for a rematch.

In Summary

Twelve trials. Twelve wins. Strength where strength was needed, cunning where strength was not enough. No labour left unfinished, and a place among the stars earned in full.

"Give him the impossible task. He will hand it back, solved, before Olympus finishes watching."
