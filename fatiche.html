The Twelve Labours of Poltro

Being a Faithful Account of Trials Endured, in the Manner of Hercules, Only With More Cables

Poltroweb bonus track number two: same Andrea as the main site and the biography, only here the myth machinery has been repurposed for software architecture, retrocomputing, and other forms of heroic overcommitment.

Every hero needs a curse to work off. Hercules had Hera's grudge and twelve impossible errands. Our protagonist has a chronic inability to say "no" to a good project, a museum, or a stray cat, and the debt has been settled, Olympian-style, in twelve labours of his own. King Eurystheus has been replaced by a rotating cast of product owners, customs officials, and eBay sellers, but the principle holds: absurd task, no way out, occasional bronze medal.

Labour I — The Nemean Lion

The original lion could not be pierced by any blade forged by mortal hands. Poltro's lion is a legacy codebase of similar temperament: no linter tames it, no changelog explains it, and every commit message from 2011 simply reads "fix." Hercules strangled his lion bare-handed; Poltro achieved the equivalent by refusing to touch the framework and instead wrapping the whole beast in an adapter layer until it stopped biting. The pelt is now worn proudly as a design pattern diagram at conferences.


Fig. 1 - The beast, subdued. Note the absence of visible claw marks and the presence of a faint, victorious smirk.

Labour II — The Lernaean Hydra

Cut off one head, two grow back. This is also an accurate description of fixing a single bug in a sufficiently ancient monorepo: close one ticket, the tracker spawns two more, each slightly more venomous than the last. Hercules needed his nephew Iolaus and a torch to cauterise the stumps. Poltro needed a colleague with a keyboard shortcut for "won't fix" and a great deal of tea. The immortal central head — the one bug nobody can reproduce — remains buried under a very large rock of a ticket labelled "P4, revisit later."

Labour III — The Ceryneian Hind

Sacred to Artemis, swift of foot, and famously exhausting to catch without giving offence. Poltro's Hind takes the form of a specific piece of vintage hardware — spotted once at a flea market, gone by the time the cash was counted, sighted again a year later on an obscure forum at three in the morning. The chase has spanned two countries and at least one customs declaration form filled out in a mixture of Italian, Russian, and hope.

[Photographic evidence pending — the Hind remains, as of this writing, at large. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 3 - The hunt continues. Reward offered in the form of enthusiastic gratitude.

Labour IV — The Erymanthian Boar

A monstrous creature that Hercules was instructed to bring back alive, which turned out to be the hard part. Poltro's Boar is any museum donation that arrives without documentation, without a power cable, and without the faintest idea of what voltage it expects. Bringing it back "alive" means getting it to boot at all, ideally without involving the fire brigade. Success is declared the moment a single LED blinks green instead of, historically, orange and then nothing.

Labour V — The Augean Stables

Thirty years of manure, cleaned in a single day by diverting two rivers. Poltro's version involves no rivers, but does involve thirty years of technical debt, cable spaghetti, and "temporary" storage boxes in a museum back room, cleared out in a single legendary weekend with nothing but labelled bins, a very long extension cord, and the stubborn refusal to leave before it was done. Hercules got paid in cattle. Poltro got paid in the quiet satisfaction of a shelf that finally closes.


Fig. 5 - Before the rivers were diverted. Note the structural integrity of the pile, achieved through decades of careful neglect.

Labour VI — The Stymphalian Birds

Man-eating birds with bronze beaks, driven off with a rattle borrowed from Athena. Poltro's flock takes the form of push notifications, calendar pop-ups, and the particular species of bird that nests in the eaves of a museum building and takes a personal, ongoing interest in the roof. The rattle, in this case, is a well-timed "do not disturb" toggle and, on bad days, an actual broom.


Fig. 6 - The flock, unimpressed by the rattle. Negotiations remain ongoing.

Labour VII — The Cretan Bull

A bull that terrorised Crete before Hercules wrestled it into submission and dragged it home by sheer stubbornness. Poltro's Bull is any conference-room AV setup encountered for the first time: three remotes, one HDMI switch of unknown loyalty, and a projector that only responds to a very specific sequence of button presses passed down as oral tradition. It is wrestled, eventually, into displaying a single slide, to general applause.


Fig. 7 - The bull, subdued mid-presentation. Round two begins the moment someone touches the cables again.

Labour VIII — The Mares of Diomedes

Man-eating horses, tamed by feeding them their own owner. A drastic solution, but an effective one. Poltro's Mares are the four overlapping deadlines that arrive every quarter, each convinced it is the most important and each willing to consume anything — sleep, weekends, patience — placed in front of it. The taming strategy is less mythologically violent and involves mostly a shared calendar, brutal prioritisation, and the occasional sacrifice of a Saturday.

[Photographic evidence pending — the Mares do not sit still for portraits. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 8 - Tamed, for now. The stable door remains reinforced.

Labour IX — The Belt of Hippolyta

A diplomatic errand that went sideways through misunderstanding and pride, ending, regrettably, in conflict. Poltro's Belt is any piece of vintage equipment that must be obtained across a border, which means an afternoon spent explaining, in careful bureaucratic prose, to a customs official why a thirty-year-old computer with no resale value is nonetheless precious cargo. Unlike the myth, no war resulted, though several forms were filled out in triplicate, which is arguably worse.


Fig. 9 - The belt, or rather the customs receipt, safely retrieved. Diplomacy narrowly averted a second labour.

Labour X — The Cattle of Geryon

Herding the cattle of a three-bodied giant across the known world, back to Eurystheus, without losing a single cow. Poltro's cattle are three cloud instances in three different regions, each convinced it is the primary one, each drifting slowly out of configuration sync the moment nobody is looking. The herding process involves infrastructure-as-code, a great deal of patience, and the firm conviction that "it works on my region" is not an acceptable final answer.


Fig. 10 - The herd, briefly in sync. A snapshot taken before configuration drift could set in again.

Labour XI — The Apples of the Hesperides

Golden apples, guarded by a hundred-headed dragon, in a garden at the edge of the world. Poltro's apples take the form of an extremely rare original component — a ROM chip, a manual, a board revision that only three people on the internet claim ever existed — guarded not by a dragon but by a bidding war on an auction site that ends, with cruel precision, at 2 a.m. local time. The garden is won, occasionally, and the apples take pride of place on a museum shelf, gleaming.


Fig. 11 - The garden at the edge of the world, or possibly just a well-lit shelf. The apples, either way, are golden.

Labour XII — Cerberus

The final labour: descend into the underworld and drag back its three-headed guardian, using no weapon but bare hands. Poltro's underworld is any government office at closing time, and his Cerberus is the three-headed bureaucratic form that must be filed in person, stamped in triplicate, and defended against a queue that behaves, unmistakably, like a hound with more heads than patience. Hercules returned Cerberus to the underworld once Eurystheus had seen enough. Poltro simply keeps the stamped copy, framed, as proof it can be done exactly once and should never be attempted again.

[Photographic evidence pending — some victories are witnessed only by a numbered ticket stub. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 12 - The final labour, unphotographed but not unfelt. The underworld, notably, did not ask for a rematch.

In Summary

Hercules earned immortality and a place among the stars. Poltro earned a stable Wi-Fi connection, a museum that mostly boots on the first try, and a shelf of golden apples nobody is allowed to touch. Twelve labours, zero divine intervention, and an unconfirmed number still pending — Eurystheus, in the form of a shared calendar invite, is already drafting the thirteenth.

"Some heroes clear rivers through stables. This one clears tickets through backlogs. The principle, and the smell of the aftermath, is largely the same."
