The Twelve Labours of Poltro

Being a Faithful Account of Trials Endured, in the Manner of Hercules, Only With More Cables and Considerably Less Toga

Poltroweb bonus track number two: same Andrea as the main site and the biography, only here the myth machinery has been repurposed for museums, borders, blades, birds, and one very unimpressed cat.

Every hero needs a curse to work off. Hercules had Hera's grudge and twelve impossible errands. Our protagonist has a chronic inability to say "no" to a good cause, a rare artefact, or a duel, and the debt has been settled, Olympian-style, in twelve labours of his own. King Eurystheus has been replaced by a rotating cast of customs officials, museum donors, fencing opponents, and one cat who considers herself co-management, but the principle holds: absurd task, no way out, occasional bronze medal.

Labour I — The Nemean Lion

The original lion could not be pierced by any blade forged by mortal hands. Poltro's lion is the heaviest CRT cabinet in the MUPIN collection, a beast that has defeated two hand trucks, one freight lift, and the combined optimism of three volunteers. Hercules strangled his lion bare-handed; Poltro achieved the equivalent with a dolly, a great deal of grunting, and the firm conviction that a museum without at least one immovable object lacks proper gravitas. The pelt is now worn, figuratively, as bragging rights at every donor visit.


Fig. 1 - The beast, subdued. Note the absence of visible claw marks and the presence of a faint, victorious smirk.

Labour II — The Lernaean Hydra

Cut off one head, two grow back. This is also an accurate description of the paperwork required to register a foreign resident across two bureaucracies at once: answer one form from Turin, and the Moscow side sprouts two more requiring a stamp only obtainable, naturally, in Turin. Hercules needed his nephew Iolaus and a torch to cauterise the stumps. Poltro needed a folder of certified translations, a great deal of tea, and the calm resignation of a man who has accepted that the immortal central head — the one form that references a document that no longer exists — will simply have to stay buried under a filing cabinet.

Labour III — The Ceryneian Hind

Sacred to Artemis, swift of foot, and famously exhausting to catch without giving offence. Poltro's Hind takes the form of a specific piece of vintage hardware — spotted once at a flea market, gone by the time the cash was counted, sighted again a year later on an obscure forum at three in the morning. The chase has spanned two countries, three languages, and at least one customs declaration form filled out in a mixture of Italian, Russian, and hope. Unlike Artemis's deer, this one does not have golden horns, but it does have a serial number that has become something of an obsession.

[Photographic evidence pending — the Hind remains, as of this writing, at large. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 3 - The hunt continues. Reward offered in the form of enthusiastic gratitude and a museum credit line.

Labour IV — The Erymanthian Boar

A monstrous creature that Hercules was instructed to bring back alive, which turned out to be the hard part. Poltro's Boar shows up twice a season on the fencing strip: an opponent built like a bear, twice his reach, and entirely unbothered by footwork subtlety. Bringing this one back "alive" means surviving the bout with both blade and dignity intact, which is achieved less through force than through patience, distance, and the occasional well-timed retreat that looks, from a distance, almost intentional.

[Photographic evidence pending — the Boar does not pause for photographs mid-bout. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 4 - The Boar, mid-bout, considerably larger than advertised. Surrender is, of course, entirely theatrical.

Labour V — The Augean Stables

Thirty years of manure, cleaned in a single day by diverting two rivers. Poltro's version involves no rivers, but does involve thirty years of donated computers, cable spaghetti, and "temporary" storage boxes in a museum back room, cleared out in a single legendary weekend with nothing but labelled bins, a very long extension cord, a supervising cat perched on the one clear shelf, and the stubborn refusal to leave before it was done. Hercules got paid in cattle. Poltro got paid in the quiet satisfaction of a door that finally closes.


Fig. 5 - Before the rivers were diverted. Note the structural integrity of the pile, achieved through decades of careful neglect.

Labour VI — The Stymphalian Birds

Man-eating birds with bronze beaks, driven off with a rattle borrowed from Athena. Poltro's flock is considerably less mythological and considerably more literal: a colony of pigeons that has taken a proprietary interest in the museum's roof, the fencing club's courtyard, and, on one memorable occasion, an open window during a donor visit. The rattle, in this case, is a broom, a strategically placed owl decoy, and the cat, who watches the whole operation from a windowsill with an air of professional disapproval.


Fig. 6 - The flock, unimpressed by the rattle, the broom, or the owl. Negotiations remain ongoing.

Labour VII — The Cretan Bull

A bull that terrorised Crete before Hercules wrestled it into submission and dragged it home by sheer stubbornness. Poltro's Bull is the ticket window at a certain provincial office, encountered on a specific day of the month, guarded by a queue system that dispenses numbers with the implacable indifference of a Cretan monster. It is wrestled, eventually, into yielding a single stamped document, to the quiet applause of everyone still waiting.


Fig. 7 - The bull, subdued at last. Round two begins the moment the ticket machine is fed again.

Labour VIII — The Mares of Diomedes

Man-eating horses, tamed by feeding them their own owner. A drastic solution, but an effective one. Poltro's Mares are the four commitments that arrive every month with equal urgency — a fencing tournament, a museum opening, a TuttiConnessi delivery of refurbished laptops, and a flight between Turin and Moscow that never quite lines up with any of the above — each one convinced it deserves the whole weekend. The taming strategy is less mythologically violent and involves mostly a shared calendar, a great deal of colour-coding, and the occasional sacrifice of a Saturday.

[Photographic evidence pending — the Mares do not sit still for portraits. Filed under "future archival acquisition."]

Fig. 8 - Tamed, for now. The stable door remains reinforced.

Labour IX — The Belt of Hippolyta

A diplomatic errand that went sideways through misunderstanding and pride, ending, regrettably, in conflict. Poltro's Belt is any piece of vintage equipment that must cross a border, which means an afternoon spent explaining, in careful and increasingly creative terms, to a customs official why a thirty-year-old computer with no resale value is nonetheless precious cargo worthy of a museum rather than a scrapyard. Unlike the myth, no war resulted, though several forms were filled out in triplicate, which is arguably worse, and the belt was won through patience rather than combat.


Fig. 9 - The belt, or rather the customs receipt, safely retrieved. Diplomacy narrowly averted a second labour.

Labour X — The Cattle of Geryon

Herding the cattle of a three-bodied giant across the known world, back to Eurystheus, without losing a single cow. Poltro's cattle are three separate lives running in parallel — the museum in Turin, the volunteers keeping TuttiConnessi's laptop pipeline alive, and whatever is currently happening in Moscow — herded together across time zones without letting any one of them wander off unattended for too long. Geryon, in this telling, is simply the shared inbox where all three occasionally collide at once.


Fig. 10 - The herd, briefly in one place at one time. A rare and treasured alignment.

Labour XI — The Apples of the Hesperides

Golden apples, guarded by a hundred-headed dragon, in a garden at the edge of the world. Poltro's apples take the form of an extremely rare original component — a ROM chip, a manual, a board revision that only three people on the internet claim ever existed — guarded not by a dragon but by a bidding war on an auction site that ends, with cruel precision, at 2 a.m. local time, whichever local time happens to apply that week. The garden is won, occasionally, and the apples take pride of place on a museum shelf, gleaming, next to a handwritten label with entirely too much punctuation.


Fig. 11 - The garden at the edge of the world, or possibly just a well-lit shelf. The apples, either way, are golden.

Labour XII — Cerberus

The final labour: descend into the underworld and drag back its three-headed guardian, using no weapon but bare hands. Poltro's underworld is any government office at closing time, and his Cerberus is the three-headed bureaucratic form that must be filed in person, stamped in triplicate, and defended against a queue that behaves, unmistakably, like a hound with more heads than patience. Hercules returned Cerberus to the underworld once Eurystheus had seen enough. Poltro simply keeps the stamped copy, framed, next to the fencing medals and the museum's first floppy disk, as proof it can be done exactly once and should never be attempted again.

Fig. 12 - The final labour, unphotographed but not unfelt. The underworld, notably, did not ask for a rematch.

In Summary

Hercules earned immortality and a place among the stars. Poltro earned a museum that mostly opens on time, a fencing rating he is quietly proud of, a stamp collection of a bureaucratic rather than philatelic nature, and a cat who has never once acknowledged any of it. Twelve labours, zero divine intervention, and an unconfirmed number still pending — Eurystheus, in the form of a shared calendar invite, is already drafting the thirteenth.

"Some heroes clear rivers through stables. This one clears customs forms, fencing bouts, and pigeon colonies, usually in the same week. The principle, and the smell of the aftermath, is largely the same."
